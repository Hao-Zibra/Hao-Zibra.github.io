A thin-film process, end to end: substrate, deposition, anneal, metrology, validation, control

The rest of this site is organised by technique. This page is organised the way a process engineer actually works: one flow, six stations, each with the failure mode that station owns and the evidence that closes it out. My own line is a sputtered metal interlayer on a ceramic electrolyte, annealed to a controlled morphology, then measured and cycled: the same substrate → deposition →  anneal → metrology → electrical → SPC loop that a fab runs on wafers.

Read this if: you hire for process, integration or yield Every station labels its evidence Unpublished steps are marked, not dressed up
How to read the evidence labels. Process fluency is easy to claim and hard to show, so every station below says exactly what backs it. PUBLIC DATA means an openly licensed dataset re-analysed here; OWN WORK means my own design or simulation, published on this site; NO PUBLIC FIGURE means the step belongs to unpublished research, described qualitatively, with no figure and no numbers, until the manuscript is out. Nothing on this page is a stand-in for something I cannot show.

The six stations

Each station: what it owns, what goes wrong there, and what I can show for it.

Station 1 Substrate preparation and fixturing OWN WORK

Everything downstream inherits the surface. A ceramic pellet is lapped and polished flat, then held in a fixture that must load it evenly, because a substrate that is clamped unevenly is, from the film's point of view, not flat at all.

Failure mode this station owns: non-uniform contact. Load that rides the rim instead of the face changes local stress by an order of magnitude, and a brittle substrate has no ductility to redistribute it.
Surface and location specified rather than assumed: datum system A/B/C, position tolerance on the tapped pattern, section through the cell pocket. This is the language a supplier and an incoming-inspection gauge both read.
And verified rather than assumed: pressure across the pellet face under a rigid platen versus a compliant interlayer, from a two-engine finite-element cross-check. The rigid case rides the rim; the interlayer restores near-uniform loading.

Station 2 PVD deposition of the interlayer NO PUBLIC FIGURE

A thin metal interlayer is sputtered onto the polished ceramic. The knobs are the familiar ones, base pressure, working gas pressure, power, throw distance, substrate condition, and they set thickness uniformity, adhesion and the film's starting microstructure.

Failure mode this station owns: a film that is nominally correct and practically useless: right average thickness, wrong uniformity or adhesion, so the defect appears three stations later as a scattered electrical result rather than as a deposition alarm.
Deliberately no figure here. The deposition work belongs to my anode-free solid-state programme in the Sakamoto group, which is manuscript in preparation. Rather than substitute a stock image or a lookalike from someone else's paper, this station stays qualitative: process reasoning without unpublished process data. What the site can show is the discipline applied to the steps that are publishable, on either side of this one.

Station 3 Anneal and morphology control NO PUBLIC FIGURE

The as-deposited film is not the film you want. A controlled thermal step under inert atmosphere does two jobs at once: it cleans the interface, and it lets the metal reorganise into the morphology that the device actually needs. Temperature, dwell and atmosphere are the recipe; the morphology is the response.

Failure mode this station owns: a thermal budget that is right for one goal and wrong for the other: enough to clean the interface but too much for the morphology, or the reverse. This is a classic process trade-off: the two objectives share one knob.
Deliberately no figure here, same reason as Station 2: manuscript in preparation. The recipe window and the resulting morphology are unpublished, so no micrographs and no numbers appear on this page.

Station 4 Metrology: turning images into numbers PUBLIC DATA

A micrograph is an opinion until someone defines the segmentation. This station is where imaging becomes measurement: denoise, segment, then extract distributions and transport descriptors with the choice of threshold written down rather than tuned until the answer looks right. The worked example below is X-ray micro-computed tomography on published open data, not SEM, AFM or XPS, which are the tools this flow would use for a thin film, and which are not shown because I have no openly licensed example of my own to show.

Failure mode this station owns: a measurement that moves when the operator changes, not when the process changes. If the segmentation is not pinned, every downstream chart is measuring the analyst.
Segmentation with a stated, reproducible criterion: the step that decides whether the numbers that follow mean anything.
Denoise before threshold, judged on a paired comparison rather than on appearance: the imaging equivalent of a gauge study.

Station 5 Electrochemical validation PUBLIC DATA OWN WORK UNPUBLISHED

A film that looks right still has to work. Impedance spectroscopy separates the interface from the bulk, so a change in the process shows up as a change in a specific arc rather than as a vague shift in cell performance, which is what makes it useful as a process response rather than just a characterisation result. On my own cells this is run operando, while the cell is cycling; that work is manuscript in preparation, so no results from it appear here. The public example below is the same method on an openly licensed dataset.

Failure mode this station owns: a validation that cannot attribute. If the test says only "worse", it cannot tell deposition from anneal from assembly, and the process team gets no direction.
Spectra resolved into separate relaxation processes, so a shift can be attributed to an interface rather than to the cell as a whole. Openly licensed dataset, re-analysed here.
The same signal built from first principles in a finite-element model, so the measurement has a physical picture behind it rather than a fitted circuit alone.

Station 6 SPC and reliability: is the process in control? PUBLIC DATA

One good part proves nothing. The last station asks whether the line is stable and what it will do over life: control charts on a build-to-build feature, drift detection on the population, and a censored life model that turns cycling into an early-life risk statement instead of a single average.

Failure mode this station owns: reacting to noise, or missing a real drift. Both look identical on a single unit and are separable only on the population.
Build stability as a control chart: limits set from the process, not from a specification, so the question becomes "did anything change?" rather than "is it in spec?".
Drift detection on individual units, with the alarm timing checked against what actually happened later. An alarm nobody can act on early enough is not an alarm.

Why this transfers

So what? The substrate is a ceramic pellet rather than a silicon wafer and the electrical test is an impedance sweep rather than a parametric probe, but the loop is the same one a fab runs: specify the incoming surface, control the deposition, spend the thermal budget where it buys the most, pin the metrology before trusting it, validate with a measurement that can attribute a failure, and then hold the whole thing with control charts. What travels between industries is not the tool set. It is the habit of asking, at every station, which failure mode this step owns and what evidence would close it out.
